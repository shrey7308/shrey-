OUTCOME · 01

History of AI

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and machine learning, and relate them to modern AI systems.

History reflection

From early ideas to generative AI

Many people think AI was created only a few years ago because ChatGPT became widely popular recently. In fact, AI has a much longer history. At the 1956 Dartmouth Conference, researchers introduced “artificial intelligence” as a field of study. Early AI systems were far less powerful than those we have today, but milestones such as IBM’s Deep Blue defeating chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997 showed what computers could accomplish.

As computers became more capable, AI advanced as well. Machine learning helped systems learn patterns from data instead of relying only on explicitly written rules. More recently, generative AI chatbots have become popular for answering questions, solving problems, writing code, and helping create images. AI continues to develop, making it important to understand both its history and how it relates to the tools we use today.

OUTCOME · 02

AI Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and machine learning, including their societal implications.

Ethics reflection

Using AI responsibly

AI ethics means making sure AI is used responsibly so it does not harm others or disproportionately affect particular groups. Unfair decisions can result from algorithmic bias in the data used to train a system. For example, a résumé screener might reject a qualified candidate because it has learned to favor certain applicants.

Another concern is calibrated trust: we should not blindly rely on AI or assume that its answers are always correct. AI systems can make mistakes, so people need to understand their limitations. AI also has the potential for dual use, when a helpful technology is repurposed for harm. Voice cloning, for instance, can help dub movies, but it can also be used to impersonate someone and commit fraud.

AI can be beneficial, but we need to recognize when it is wrong or unfair and consider how people might misuse it.

Case study · 01

The Predictive Policing Pilot

Scenario

Fairmont uses an AI system called SafeZone to predict which neighborhoods may experience more crime. It analyzes past crime data to help police decide where to deploy officers. However, arrest records can reflect where police have focused in the past, not just where crime occurs. SafeZone repeatedly sends officers to those same areas, leading to more arrests and reinforcing the pattern. This can make the system appear successful even when its predictions are not accurate. Before budget reviews, a police captain sends extra officers to these neighborhoods to make the system look effective and secure more funding.

Ethical analysis

The main concern is bias and fairness. SafeZone treats more arrests as evidence of more crime, even though arrests may increase because an area is policed more heavily. This can direct disproportionate attention toward predominantly minority neighborhoods, even when actual crime rates are not higher. Residents may feel targeted and lose trust in the police. The captain’s actions make the problem worse by using the system to pursue funding rather than serve the community. The city should audit SafeZone’s accuracy, examine how policing patterns affect its data, and avoid treating AI predictions as facts. This case shows how AI can reinforce existing problems when its data is biased.

Case study · 02

The Essay Scorer

Scenario

The Springfield School District uses an AI system called ScoreWrite to grade students’ essays on a year-end exam. The district expects the system to make grading more consistent, so many teachers begin relying on its scores instead of giving detailed feedback. A researcher later discovers that ScoreWrite gives lower scores to English language learners and students who use different dialects, even when their ideas are just as strong. The system was trained mostly on highly rated essays written in standard academic English. The district also begins using its scores to evaluate teachers, although the system was never designed for that purpose.

Ethical analysis

The main concern is bias and fairness in education. ScoreWrite favors students who write in a particular style, putting others at a disadvantage even when they have strong ideas. Lower scores can harm their grades and future opportunities. Teachers’ overreliance on the system is also a problem: students need feedback that helps them understand mistakes and improve, and a score alone may not explain what needs work. Using the scores to evaluate teachers is unfair because student performance depends on more than teaching alone. The district should test ScoreWrite on essays from students with varied backgrounds and language styles, and teachers should continue reviewing the essays themselves. Consistent scores are not necessarily fair or correct.

OUTCOME · 03

Core AI Techniques

Describe how core AI techniques, such as search and adversarial reasoning, are used to model intelligent behavior.

Techniques reflection

Search, planning, and adversarial reasoning

Core AI techniques, such as search and adversarial reasoning, model intelligent behavior by helping an agent evaluate possible states, consider different actions, and choose decisions that support its goals. Search algorithms let an agent explore possible states in an environment and find a path toward a goal.

In “Practical Search Techniques in Path Planning for Autonomous Driving,” Dolgov, Thrun, Montemerlo, and Diebel describe using A* search for Junior, an autonomous vehicle built by the Stanford Racing Team. Junior must handle tasks a regular car faces, such as navigating parking lots and avoiding obstacles. In this example, Junior is the agent, and its surroundings—from parking lots and roads to obstacles—make up the environment. Its initial state is its current location and orientation, and the goal test checks whether it has reached its destination. The search tree represents possible states explored along the way, while the search space is the set of states the vehicle could occupy.

The article describes Hybrid-State A*, a modified version of A*. When a node is expanded, the algorithm simulates steering actions to generate possible next states. It uses the evaluation function f(n) = g(n) + h(n), where g(n) is the accumulated cost of reaching the current state and h(n) estimates the remaining cost to the goal.

Adversarial reasoning is useful when agents have opposing goals. Each agent considers the other’s possible actions before choosing its own. Minimax is an adversarial search algorithm: the maximizing player chooses the highest utility value, while the minimizing player chooses the lowest. Utility values represent how favorable an outcome is for a player. Minimax evaluates possible game states and works backward from terminal states to find a strong decision. This lets an agent anticipate an opponent’s moves and choose an action that improves its outcome.

Together, search and adversarial reasoning help AI agents explore possible states, predict outcomes, account for other agents’ actions, and make decisions that move them toward their goals.

OUTCOME · 04

Machine Learning Approaches

Explain fundamental machine learning approaches and distinguish between the major learning paradigms.

Machine learning reflection

Learning through experience

Machine learning is a way of building computer systems that learn patterns from data and use them to make predictions or decisions. Rather than writing a separate rule for every situation, developers provide data and code that allow a model to learn useful patterns.

Reinforcement learning is a machine learning approach in which an agent learns to make decisions through trial and error. As it interacts with an environment, it receives rewards or penalties. Over time, it learns which actions tend to lead to higher rewards and which actions to avoid. A state represents the agent’s current situation, an action is the decision it makes, a reward is the numerical feedback it receives, and the next state is the situation that follows. The agent learns over many episodes to improve its decisions.

A key challenge is balancing exploration and exploitation. Exploration means trying different actions to discover which ones produce better results. Exploitation means choosing an action the agent currently believes will produce the highest reward. The agent needs a balance: too little exploration can cause it to miss better options, while too much can prevent it from using what it has already learned.

Q-learning is one example of reinforcement learning. Its Q-table stores estimates of how valuable different actions are in different states. The agent updates these Q-values using the rewards it receives and estimates of future rewards, helping it improve its choices over time.

OUTCOME · 05

Generative AI and Large Language Models

Describe how generative AI and large language models operate at a conceptual level, and evaluate when their use is appropriate.

Generative AI reflection

How language models generate responses

Generative AI creates new content, such as text, images, audio, and code. Large language models (LLMs) are trained on large collections of text so they can learn patterns and relationships in language. When a user enters a prompt, the text is divided into small units called tokens. The model represents tokens as numerical vectors, called embeddings. Tokens used in similar contexts tend to have related representations—for example, “happy,” “glad,” and “joyful.” This helps the model use relationships among words and the surrounding text when generating a response.

During training, a language model learns to predict tokens from context. It compares its predictions with the training text and adjusts its internal parameters to improve. When responding to a prompt, it calculates probabilities for what token might come next, selects a token, and repeats the process until it forms a complete response. ChatGPT is one example: it generates a response based on patterns learned during training and the conversation’s context.

Generative AI can help people understand topics, organize information, brainstorm, and complete tasks. However, a response can sound convincing while still containing errors. Important information should be checked against reliable sources, and AI should not be blindly trusted for high-stakes situations or life-threatening advice.

Students should also use generative AI responsibly. It can support learning, but relying on it to write essays or solve problems can get in the way of building those skills and may conflict with course rules. Overall, LLMs generate relevant responses by using patterns learned from data, but people still need to judge when and how to use them.
