OUTCOME · 01

History of AI

Explain key moments in the historical development of AI and machine learning, and relate them to modern AI systems.

History reflection

From early ideas to generative AI

Many people think AI was created only a few years ago because ChatGPT became widely popular recently. In fact, AI has a much longer history. At the 1956 Dartmouth Conference, researchers introduced “artificial intelligence” as a field of study. Early AI systems were far less powerful than those we have today, but milestones such as IBM’s Deep Blue defeating chess champion Garry Kasparov in 1997 showed what computers could accomplish.

As computers became more capable, AI advanced as well. Machine learning helped systems learn patterns from data instead of relying only on explicitly written rules. More recently, generative AI chatbots have become popular for answering questions, solving problems, writing code, and helping create images. AI continues to develop, making it important to understand both the history behind these systems and how they relate to the tools we use today.

OUTCOME · 02

AI Ethics

Identify and discuss ethical issues associated with AI and machine learning, including their societal implications.

Ethics reflection

Using AI responsibly

AI ethics means making sure AI is used responsibly so it does not harm others or disproportionately affect particular groups. Unfair decisions can result from algorithmic bias in the data used to train a system. For example, a résumé screener might reject a qualified candidate because it has learned to favor certain applicants.

Another concern is calibrated trust: we should not blindly rely on AI or assume that its answers are always correct. AI systems can make mistakes, so people need to understand their limitations. AI also has the potential for dual use, when a helpful technology is repurposed for harm. Voice cloning, for instance, can help dub movies, but it can also be used to impersonate someone and commit fraud.

AI can be beneficial, but we need to recognize when it is wrong or unfair and consider how people might misuse it.

Case study · 01

The Predictive Policing Pilot

Scenario

Fairmont uses an AI system called SafeZone to predict which neighborhoods may experience more crime. It analyzes past crime data to help police decide where to deploy officers. However, arrest records can reflect where police have focused in the past, not just where crime occurs. SafeZone repeatedly sends officers to those same areas, leading to more arrests and reinforcing the pattern. This can make the system appear successful even when its predictions are not accurate. Before budget reviews, a police captain sends extra officers to these neighborhoods to make the system look effective and secure more funding.

Ethical analysis

The main concern is bias and fairness. SafeZone treats more arrests as evidence of more crime, even though arrests may increase because an area is policed more heavily. This can direct disproportionate attention toward predominantly minority neighborhoods, even when actual crime rates are not higher. Residents may feel targeted and lose trust in the police. The captain’s actions make the problem worse by using the system to pursue funding rather than serve the community. The city should audit SafeZone’s accuracy, examine how policing patterns affect its data, and avoid treating AI predictions as facts. This case shows how AI can reinforce existing problems when its data is biased.

Case study · 02

The Essay Scorer

Scenario

The Springfield School District uses an AI system called ScoreWrite to grade students’ essays on a year-end exam. The district expects the system to make grading more consistent, so many teachers begin relying on its scores instead of giving detailed feedback. A researcher later discovers that ScoreWrite gives lower scores to English language learners and students who use different dialects, even when their ideas are just as strong. The system was trained mostly on highly rated essays written in standard academic English. The district also begins using its scores to evaluate teachers, although the system was never designed for that purpose.

Ethical analysis

The main concern is bias and fairness in education. ScoreWrite favors students who write in a particular style, putting others at a disadvantage even when they have strong ideas. Lower scores can harm their grades and future opportunities. Teachers’ overreliance on the system is also a problem: students need feedback that helps them understand mistakes and improve, and a score alone may not explain what needs work. Using the scores to evaluate teachers is unfair because student performance depends on more than teaching alone. The district should test ScoreWrite on essays from students with varied backgrounds and language styles, and teachers should continue reviewing the essays themselves. Consistent scores are not necessarily fair or correct.
